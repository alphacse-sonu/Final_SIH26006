Read this first

Every freight-rate history, port draft limit, berth-availability figure and weather probability in this repository is synthetic demo data, generated by a seeded simulator in backend/data/generate_synthetic_data.py. It is not Baltic Exchange data, not Clarksons data and not live port information. The models, the engineering and the decision logic are real and inspectable; the numbers they are fitted to are placeholders. Section 14 quantifies exactly how far the current outputs can be trusted.

1. Executive Summary

What the platform is, the five questions it answers, and why it is built the way it is.

1.1 The one-paragraph version

A chartering desk at SAIL receives a parcel of coal or steel, an origin region and an East Coast discharge port. Historically the answer came from a broker's phone call and a spot fixture. This platform replaces that with a single HTTP call that runs six analytical modules in sequence, each one consuming the previous one's output, and returns one connected recommendation: which ship, at what delivered cost, chartered or spot, entered when.

1.2 The five questions, and the module that answers each

#QuestionAnswered byOutput
1When should we charter?models/forecasting.py90-day rate forecast with 80% and 95% quantile confidence bands
2What does the voyage truly cost?models/cost_calculator.pyEight-component delivered cost, not just the freight quote
3Which ship can actually do it?models/vessel_optimizer.pyFeasible vessel chosen from parcel size + both ports' draft, LOA, beam and handling limits — or a multi-voyage plan
4What if the ship is too deep?models/lightering.pyTPC-integrated lightering tonnage, STS cost, weather-gated recommendation
5Charter or spot?models/decision_engine.pyNPV comparison, breakeven rate, 1,000-path Monte Carlo, 95% VaR, timing and idle signals
—How risky is the whole plan?main.py :: _calculate_risk_summaryWeighted 0–100 composite across weather, congestion and volatility

The user never selects a vessel class. The optimizer derives it, and every downstream module then consumes that derived vessel — this is the single most important architectural decision in the system.

1.3 Why the modules are connected rather than separate

Most "AI" tools in this space return a rate number and stop. The modules here are chained so that each one narrows the problem for the next: the optimizer decides the parcel size per voyage, which sets the forecaster's cargo basis; the forecaster's day-1 rate becomes the cost engine's freight input; the optimizer's chosen class becomes the lightering module's hull; and the forecast's confidence band becomes the decision engine's simulation variance. Change the parcel and the entire chain re-derives.

1Constraint-first

Vessel feasibility is resolved before economics. A 180,000 t Capesize on Newcastle→Paradip is rejected on LOA and beam at Newcastle, not scored and rejected on price.

2Full delivered cost

Freight is typically 60–65% of delivered cost. Judging a charter on the freight quote alone is judging 35% of the decision.

3Explicit uncertainty

Every forecast carries a distribution, not a point. The Monte Carlo propagates that distribution into a probability and a VaR, so the desk sees the downside before committing.

2. Problem Statement — PS-26006

Why SAIL asked for this, and the operational pain it replaces.

2.1 Context

SAIL procures bulk raw material — coking coal and steel — on long-term contracts from overseas origins (Australia, the US Gulf, Mozambique, Russia, Indonesia) into Indian East Coast discharge ports. The freight and chartering decision sits between the commercial contract and the physical voyage. The stated problem is that chartering is reactive and one-off: each fixture is negotiated in isolation, at the moment cargo is ready, with no forward view of where rates are heading.

2.3 Scope boundary — what the problem statement does not ask for

  • No live market feed. The platform consumes a modelled rate series. Wiring a Baltic Exchange or SAIL approved feed is an integration task, not a modelling one — the forecaster's input contract is features_<ROUTE>.csv.
  • No cargoes-fixture matching algorithm. The platform optimises the transport decision downstream of a cargo contract, not the counterparty selection itself.
  • No legal or charter-party drafting. Time charter versus voyage charter is modelled purely as a discounted cash-flow comparison of the freight leg.
Design consequence

Because the rate feed is modelled, every downstream module is written against a stable interface rather than against a specific data vendor. Replacing the synthetic series with approved data is a file substitution plus a re-run of the generator, not an architectural change. This was a deliberate design choice, and it is also the reason the current numerical outputs must not be presented as market intelligence.

4. The Data Layer

Everything downstream is a function of this layer. It is also where the system's largest caveat lives.

4.1 Synthetic market generator

generate_synthetic_data.py builds five years (1,825 days, 2020-01-01 → 2024-12-29) of daily observations with four superimposed components. The whole process is seeded with np.random.seed(42), so the dataset is byte-identical on every regeneration — which is what allows the forecast numbers in this document to be reproducible.

4.1.1 Mean-reverting core — Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

Freight rates are modelled as a discrete Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) diffusion, the standard first-order model for a mean-reverting spread or level:

EQ 4.1 r(t) = r(t−1) + θ(μ − r(t−1)) + σ(t)·ε(t)
where   ε(t) ~ 𝒩(0, 1)  and  r(t) ≥ 0.2μ  (floor applied)
r(t) freight rate, USD/tonne
μ long-run mean = lane's historical_avg_rate
θ mean-reversion speed = 0.03 for every lane
σ(t) conditional volatility from the GARCH state
ε(t) standard normal shock
Half-life ln2/0.03 ≈ 23 days

A half-life of roughly 23 days is the reason the generated series look so smooth: deviations from the mean decay fast, and the rate is pulled back toward historical_avg_rate almost every week. For AUS_PAR the realised five-year statistics are a mean of $12.96/t inside a range of $12.34 – $13.61. Section 14 explains why this matters for model evaluation.

4.1.2 Volatility clustering — GARCH(1,1)

Conditional variance follows a GARCH(1,1) recursion, which is what produces the "calm then turbulent" texture real freight markets have and which a constant-variance simulator cannot:

EQ 4.2 h(t) = α0 + α1·εt−12 + β1·h(t−1),    σ(t) = √h(t)
α0 = 0.05·h(0) — intercept
α1 = 0.10 — ARCH reaction to last shock
β1 = 0.85 — persistence of prior variance
α1+β1 = 0.95 < 1 → covariance-stationary

The sum α₁ + β₁ = 0.95 is the stationarity condition. At 0.95 the implied variance shock persistence half-life is ≈ 13.5 trading days, which is the correct order of magnitude for a bulk freight volatility cluster.

4.1.3 Seasonality — Fourier harmonics

Two sinusoidal harmonics are layered in as daily increments, one anchored on the origin region's demand peak and one on a secondary peak (northern-hemisphere winter, or the southern-hemisphere equivalent for Australia/Indonesia/Mozambique):

EQ 4.3 S(t) = [ A1·sin(2π(t − 30.44(m1−3))/365) + A2·sin(2π(t − 30.44(m2−3))/365) ] × 0.01

4.1.4 Co-movement with BDI and bunkers

EQ 4.4 r(t) = r(t−1) + θ(μ−r(t−1)) + S(t) + σ(t)ε(t) + βbdi·ΔBDI(t) + βbnr·ΔB(t)

with β_bdi = 0.002·μ/1500 (a rising dry index lifts the freight rate) and β_bunker = −0.001·μ/580 (negative — a higher fuel price compresses the netback the shipper can pay). That sign convention is the correct economic one and is worth calling out because it is frequently got wrong in naive simulators.

5. Module 1 — Freight Rate Forecasting

A three-member ensemble with genuine quantile-based confidence intervals, in backend/models/forecasting.py.

5.1 Why an ensemble, and why this specific combination

MemberRoleJustification
XGBoost
reg:squarederror		Point estimate, member ½300 trees, depth 6, lr 0.05. Captures the feature interactions that dominate tabular shipping data (e.g. "high BDI matters only when momentum is positive").
XGBoost
reg:quantileerror		Confidence bounds ×4Trained separately at α = 0.025, 0.10, 0.90, 0.975. Each estimates a conditional quantile of the distribution, not a symmetric error bar. This is the only component that makes the intervals honest.
LightGBMPoint estimate, member ½Leaf-wise growth and a different binning scheme produce decorrelated errors from XGBoost, so the 50/50 average genuinely reduces variance rather than re-weighting one model.
LSTM
(PyTorch)		Residual correction, h ≤ 7 dTrained on the residuals of the tree ensemble, not on the raw series. A tree ensemble cannot represent smooth sequential patterns in what is left over; an LSTM can. Decay factor 1−(h−1)/7 fades it out by day 7.
EQ 5.1 — final point forecast ŷ(h) = 0.5·XGBmed(h) + 0.5·LGBM(h) + 1[h≤7] · LSTM(h) · (1 − (h−1)/7)

5.3 Training procedure

#StepDetail
1Load features_<ROUTE>.csv1,705 rows. Raises FileNotFoundError with an actionable message if the generator has not been run.
2Chronological 80/20 splitsplit_idx = int(0.8·1705) = 1364. Train 1,364 · validation 341. No shuffling — a shuffled split would leak future information into the training set and inflate the reported error.
3Standardise featuresStandardScaler fitted on train only, applied to validation. Required for the LSTM; harmless and mildly helpful for the trees.
4Fit XGBoost median300 estimators, depth 6, lr 0.05, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8, seed 42, with eval_set for early-stopping signal.
5Fit 4 quantile XGBoosts200 estimators, depth 5, lr 0.05. α = 0.10 / 0.90 → 80% band; α = 0.025 / 0.975 → 95% band.
6Fit LightGBM300 estimators, depth 6, lr 0.05, same subsampling, seed 42.

5.4 Confidence interval mathematics

Interval half-widths come from the quantile models and are then scaled by √h for multi-step horizons:

EQ 5.2 W80(h) = [ Q0.90(xh) − Q0.10(xh) ] · √h
W95(h) = [ Q0.975(xh) − Q0.025(xh) ] · √h

The √h factor is not arbitrary. For a random walk, the variance of an h-step forecast error is h times the one-step variance, so the standard deviation grows as √h. Applying the same reasoning to a quantile spread gives the widening above. Empirically on AUS_PAR the 95% band grows from $13.36–$13.45 at day 1 to $12.09–$14.63 at day 30 and $11.16–$15.55 at day 90 — a band of 0.67% → 19.0% → 32.9% of the point forecast. The bands are correctly ordered (95% ⊃ 80%) and correctly widening.

Caveat on the multi-step path

The forecast is generated by iterating the one-step model. Only the rate_lag_1 column is refreshed between steps; the other 51 features are frozen at their last observed values. The consequence is visible in the output: day 1 = $13.40, day 3 = $13.36, and days 5–90 all sit at $13.36. The point path is effectively flat after day 3. The confidence bands continue to widen correctly, so the interval is informative even though the central path is not. Section 14.3 records this as the highest-priority modelling fix.

6. Module 2 — Vessel Optimizer

Constraint-first vessel selection and multi-voyage planning, in backend/models/vessel_optimizer.py. Runs in under 10 ms — it contains no model inference.

6.1 Design principle: gates, then scores

The optimizer never ranks an infeasible ship. It applies five hard gates that produce a rejection list with human-readable reasons, and only then scores the survivors. This is why the API can return "Capesize (Newcastlemax) rejected: LOA 300 m exceeds route limit 250 m" — the desk learns why the obvious large ship is unavailable, not merely that it lost.

6.2 The five feasibility gates

GateConstraintRule and rationale
G1Parcel vs capacityQ ≤ 0.95 · DWT. The 5% margin is an explicit operational reserve — the code comment records it as preventing a recommendation "at 100% DWT".
G2Loaded draftd(Q) ≤ min(d_origin, d_dest). The minimum of the two ports binds, because a ship must be able to enter both. This single gate is what excludes Capesize from most PS-26006 lanes.
G3LOALOA ≤ min(LOA_origin, LOA_dest) — berth and approach-channel length.
G4BeamB ≤ min(B_origin, B_dest) — berth width and channel constraint.

6.2.1 Draft estimation model

Draft is interpolated linearly between the vessel's light draft (first TPC-curve point) and its maximum designed draft, scaled by the load factor:

EQ 6.1 λ = min(1, Q / DWT)     d(Q) = dmin + (dmax − dmin) · λ

A real hydrostatic calculation would integrate the vessel's actual waterplane area against cargo weight. This linear surrogate is monotone, correctly bounded, and cheap — adequate for a feasibility screen, and the reason the same function can be reused by the lightering module without inconsistency.

6.4 Multi-voyage planning

When no single hull clears gate G1, the optimizer does not give up. It computes the largest parcel each hull can carry within the route draft limit, then solves for the voyage count:

EQ 6.3 fd = min(1, (dlim − dmin)/(dmax − dmin))
Cvoy = min(0.95·DWT, DWT·fd)
n = ⌈ Q / Cvoy ⌉     per-voyage parcel = Q/n

The split-mode score re-weights the objective to suit the new problem:

EQ 6.4 Ssplit = 100 · [ 0.45·(1/n) + 0.30·M + 0.15·U + 0.10·T ]

Minimising voyage count is now the primary term at 45% weight — a two-voyage plan on bigger ships beats a five-voyage plan on Handysizes even at worse material fit. Candidates sort by (split_voyages, −feasibility_score): fewest voyages first.

7.5 Known weaknesses in the cost model

  • Waiting cost is charged on both ports for the full voyage. It is an expected-value line, not a schedule simulation, and it treats a load-side wait and a discharge-side wait identically. At 12.3% of total it deserves a proper queueing or agent-based treatment before production use.
  • Demurrage is a deterministic threshold, not a distribution. Real demurrage is a function of berth-window variance, which the waiting model already hints at but does not couple.
  • Insurance is a flat 0.2% with no war-risk, sanctions or lay-up loading.
  • No cargo-handling cost (stevedoring, tally, shunting) — usually borne by the charterer and often larger than insurance.
  • No ballast or repositioning cost, which matters directly for the idle-management question in §9.6.
  • No time-value treatment inside the voyage; everything is undiscounted and evaluated at fixture date.

8. Module 4 — Lightering Optimizer

Ship-to-ship cargo transfer solved from the vessel's TPC curve, in backend/models/lightering.py.

8.1 The naval-architecture problem

A loaded ship draws more water than an empty one. If her loaded draft exceeds the discharge berth's charted depth, she must transfer cargo into a lighter at anchor until she draws less. The quantity to transfer is not proportional to the draft excess, because the ship becomes more buoyant-per-centimetre as she rises toward the waterline. That non-linearity is exactly what the TPC curve encodes.

TPC — Tonnes Per Centimetre

The immersion (weight) required to sink a vessel one further centimetre, as a function of her current draft. It is the first derivative of displacement with respect to draft — the discrete form of the waterplane area A(d). Because A(d) widens as a hull approaches its parallel middle body, TPC rises with draft. In this dataset TPC rises about 20% from light to fully loaded on every hull — which is exactly why a linear draft-to-tonnage conversion would under-transfer.

8.2 The integration

EQ 8.1 dtarget = dport − mUKC   ← 0.5 m under-keel clearance by default

W = ∫dtargetdloaded TPC(d) dd · 100

The ×100 converts metres to centimetres. The integral is evaluated as a left-endpoint Riemann sum at 1 cm resolution:

EQ 8.2 W = Σk=0 … n−1 TPC(dtarget + k/100)  ,   n = ⌊100·(dloaded − dtarget)⌋

At 1 cm resolution the discretisation error against a true integral of a piecewise-linear TPC curve is bounded by the TPC slope over one step — under 0.1 t on these hulls. The code additionally reports avg_tpc = W/n, which is the quantity a naval architect would sanity-check first, and returns the first 10 TPC samples as a tpc_profile for plotting.

9. Module 5 — Charter vs Spot Decision Engine

NPV comparison, analytic breakeven, and 1,000-path Monte Carlo with Value-at-Risk, in backend/models/decision_engine.py.

9.1 NPV of the two cash-flow streams

EQ 9.1 NPV(X) = Σt=1T CFt / (1 + r/m)t
CFt = cost paid in period t
r = 8% p.a. default discount rate
m = 12 periods/year (monthly)
T = contract months (1–24)
Charter CF = R·Q, constant
Spot CF = St·Q, St = forecast

Daily forecasts are aggregated into contract months in 30-day blocks, averaging both the point forecast and the two CI bounds. If contract_months exceeds the 90-day forecast horizon, remaining months are extrapolated flat from the last value with the band widened 15% per extra month.

9.2 Breakeven charter rate — closed form

Setting the two NPVs equal and cancelling the parcel size Q, which appears on both sides:

EQ 9.2 Σ R·Q/(1+r)t = Σ St·Q/(1+r)t
⇒ R* = Σ (St/(1+r)t) ÷ Σ (1/(1+r)t)

So the breakeven rate is the discount-factor-weighted average of the forecast spot rates. Its practical value to the desk is that it is a negotiating number: "we will not sign above $13.36/t", derived from the model rather than asserted.

If the user supplies no proposed rate, the engine defaults to 0.95 × R* — a deliberately charter-favourable stance, on the reasoning that a recommendation engine should model the case for the decision it is being asked to evaluate rather than assume a null result.

A structural artefact in the current verdict

The 93.3% probability of charter winning is not a market insight. It is close to the arithmetic consequence of three design choices interacting: (i) the auto-proposed rate is set 5% below breakeven; (ii) the forecast path is nearly flat (all six monthly rates = $13.36), so the simulated distribution is tight around a known number; (iii) σ is derived from the 95% band, which the √h widening keeps moderate. A flat forecast plus a rate 5% below its own mean will win roughly 90–95% of the time almost regardless of the market. The verdict is arithmetically correct given the inputs and should be reported as such. On a real, volatile rate series the probability would be materially lower and far more informative. The module also reports the best 7-day window — the position of the rolling-mean minimum and its average rate — so the desk receives an actionable date range rather than a general caution.

9.6.2 Idle-scenario management

Contiguous runs of the rolling mean at or below Q25 are extracted as low-demand windows, the three cheapest retained. A low rate is used as a proxy for softer freight demand — reasonable in a market where weak rates and thin cargo coexist, though it is a proxy and not an observed cargo-volume signal.

The emitted strategy has three concrete parts: (a) avoid unnecessary ballast repositioning during soft windows; (b) keep the vessel near the relevant loading region where commercially practical; (c) evaluate an alternate employment on another modelled East Coast lane before committing to a long idle spell. Part (c) is the one that requires data the platform does not yet have — see §14.5.

13.2 The chain of reasoning

#Question the module asksAnswer produced
1Can any single hull carry 120,000 t into Newcastle and Paradip? No. All 7 hulls fail gate G1 (95% usable capacity). Newcastle's LOA 250 m and beam 38 m independently eliminate both Capesizes. Kamsarmax wins on 0.45/0.30/0.15/0.10 split scoring (score 72.4). Alternatives: Panamax Std, Supramax Ultramax, Supramax Std, Handysize.
2What will this lane cost over the next 90 days? Trains the AUS_PAR ensemble on 1,364 rows (5.6 s). Last observed rate $13.52/t. Day 1 forecast $13.40, day 3 $13.36, flat thereafter. 95% band $13.36–13.45 (d1) → $12.09–14.63 (d30) → $11.16–15.55 (d90). Validation MAE $0.0357.
3What is the true delivered cost of one 60,000 t voyage? Freight $804,000 + port $228,000 + waiting $184,908 + weather $15,360 + insurance $14,400 + bunker $257,520 = $1,504,188 per voyage. × 2 voyages = $3,008,377 ⇒ $25.07/t delivered against a $13.40/t freight quote. The delivered cost is 87% higher than the freight line — and 46.5% of it is invisible in the quote.
4Is there a cheaper feasible hull? Prices all 5 feasible hulls at the 60,000 t per-voyage parcel (not at each hull's own maximum). Panamax Std and Supramax Std both at $21.22/t, Kamsarmax and Ultramax at $21.26/t, Handysize $22.12/t — but the optimizer's Kamsarmax is retained, because selection_basis states feasibility and fit decide the vessel, and the cost spread is inside rounding. Correctly not overridden by a 0.2% price difference.
5Can the Kamsarmax enter Paradip? Loaded draft 12.76 m vs Paradip 18.5 m. Comfortable. DIRECT_BERTH, no lightering, no weather gate engaged — so weather_risk is null and the risk module falls back to its default weather score of 50.
6Charter at $12.69/t, or keep spot? Breakeven $13.36/t. NPV: charter $4,464,488 vs spot $4,699,965. CHARTER · HIGH confidence, P = 93.3%, expected saving $235,478, VaR95 −$27,058, worst case −$368,654. Timing signal WAIT_FOR_WINDOW (current 7-day mean $13.37 sits at the upper quartile).
7How risky overall? Volatility 25 · Weather 50 (default) · Congestion 100 ⇒ overall 52 · MODERATE. The binding risk is 11.6 days of berth waiting at Newcastle and Paradip, not the freight market.

13.3 The recommendation, assembled

Parcel 120,000 t coal · Newcastle → Paradip · June loading · 6-month contract

14. Limitations & Validity Register

What this system genuinely demonstrates, what it does not, and what must change before deployment.

The single most important sentence in this document

The engineering is real; the data is synthetic; the numerical outputs are therefore demonstrations of mechanism, not statements about the freight market. Nothing in Sections 5, 7 or 9 should be quoted as a market forecast, a price expectation or a risk quantification until the data layer is replaced with SAIL-approved historical series and the models are re-validated on them.

14.1 Data-layer limitations

#IssueConsequence and required action
D1All history is syntheticFive years of OU-generated rates with no true regime structure, no freight-market crises, no structural breaks. Action: ingest Baltic Exchange / Clarksons / SAIL fixture history. The forecaster's input contract makes this a data task, not a code change.
D2Rate variance is far too smallAUS_PAR spans $12.34–$13.61 over five years (±2.5%). Real coal freight on this lane has moved by factors of 3–5. The narrow band is what makes MAE look excellent. Action: calibrate σ and the OU half-life to observed data; expect every accuracy metric to worsen by an order of magnitude, which would be more believable.
D3Data is staleThe series ends 2024-12-29. As of this document's date that is roughly 21 months old, so even as a demo the "current rate" is not current. Action: regenerate with an end date near the present.
D4Port figures are syntheticDrafts, berth availability, handling rates, tariffs and weather probabilities are all plausible but unverified. Two visible tells: Sagar-Sandheads is listed under "India" with a 210 m LOA, and 21 of the 33 ports serve no lane. Action: replace with port authority / World Port Source data and re-derive the constraint tables.
D5Data currency is decoupled from the modelgenerate_synthetic_data.py writes the CSVs but nothing invalidates a cached forecaster when the underlying file changes. Editing a feature file and re-requesting the same lane serves stale predictions until the process restarts. Action: key the cache on a file mtime hash.

14.2 Modules that are unreachable or dormant in the current configuration

#IssueConsequence and required action
M1Canal fee logic is dead codeAll 35 lanes have canal_transit: null, so the Suez and Panama tariff functions — including both full tiered schedules — can never execute. Verified: 0 of 35 lanes carry a canal. Correct for these origin/destination pairs (none routes via Suez or Panama), but it means ~25 lines of tariff logic are untested. Action: add one canal-transiting lane as a regression test.
M2Weather risk inverts availability on 89% of lanesThe weather assessment lives inside the lightering module and only runs when lightering is required. On the 89% of combinations needing no transfer, weather_risk is null, so the risk module silently substitutes its default 50. Weather is never actually assessed for the voyage. Action: hoist weather assessment out of lightering.py into a port-level function called unconditionally.
M3Lightering cost is excluded from total costThe README claims nine cost components; the summation contains eight. On a lightering voyage the delivered-cost total understates reality by the whole STS operation (up to ~$94k on the Capesize→Gopalpur case). Action: add lightering_cost.total_cost_usd to the total when the module recommends LIGHTER, and reconcile the README.
M4Pie chart is inconsistent on split voyagesOn a FEASIBLE_SPLIT result the cost total is multiplied by the voyage count but the breakdown rows are not. The table correctly prints a per-voyage note; the donut chart does not, so its slices sum to half the displayed total. Action: scale the pie's dataKey values by voyage_plan.voyages.
M57-day rolling mean degenerates on flat forecastsWhen the forecast path is near-constant, the 25th and 75th percentiles of the rolling mean collapse to the same value, so the timing classifier is decided by floating-point noise and reports contradictory states. Observed on AUS_PAR: WAIT_FOR_WINDOW while simultaneously claiming the best window averages the same $13.36 as every other window. Action: require a minimum relative spread (e.g. 0.5%) before emitting a directional signal, else return MONITOR.

14.3 Modelling limitations

#IssueConsequence and required action
MO1Multi-step forecast is effectively flatOnly rate_lag_1 is refreshed between recursive steps; the other 51 features are frozen at their last observed values, so the model reaches a fixed point by day 3. Verified: days 5–90 all return $13.36. Action: implement a proper recursive feature-update loop that rolls every lag and recomputes the rolling statistics, or train a direct multi-horizon model per horizon. This is the highest-priority modelling fix in the system.
MO2Low accuracy is an artefact of the data, not skillGain concentrates in rate_rolling_max_7 (61.6%) and rate_lag_1 (60.3%); BDI, bunker and all Fourier terms contribute ≈ 0. The model learned that tomorrow ≈ today, which is near-optimal on a narrow OU series. The 0.274% MAPE is therefore not a claim about freight predictability. Action: report accuracy only after backtesting on real data, and publish a naive-persistence benchmark alongside it.
MO3LSTM contribution is unverified and probably negativeValidation metrics are computed on the tree ensemble only; the LSTM residual term is added at inference but never scored. It also receives a single time step at inference (seq_len = 1) despite being trained on 30-step sequences — a train/serve shape mismatch. Because a residual corrector with an untrained-on-real-data target can easily inject noise, it may be subtracting value. Action: score the full ensemble including the LSTM term, and either fix the sequence construction or drop the layer.
MO4Monte Carlo ignores serial correlationThe 1,000 paths draw each month independently. Real freight rates are strongly autocorrelated, so true multi-month variance is materially larger than simulated and VaR is understated. Action: sample a correlated path — a multivariate normal with the empirical residual correlation matrix, or a bootstrap over historical monthly returns.
MO5Monte Carlo is not reproduciblemonte_carlo_simulation() calls np.random.normal with no seed. Two identical requests return different P(charter cheaper) values — observed 93.3% and 94.3% on the same input. Action: seed from the route code, or expose a seed in the request schema.
MO6Breakeven is degenerate when the forecast is flatWith a constant forecast, R* reduces to that constant — the discounting has nothing to differentiate. The weighted-average formula is correct; its input is currently uninformative. Action: resolves automatically once MO1 is fixed.
MO7The 0.95 R* default biases toward CHARTERWhen no rate is proposed, the engine models a 5% discount to its own breakeven, so the charter case starts with a built-in advantage. Defensible as a negotiating posture, but it must be labelled as such in the UI rather than presented as a neutral finding. Action: make the default explicit in the response and in the panel copy.
MO8No walk-forward or rolling backtestA single chronological 80/20 split is the only evaluation. There is no multi-origin rolling-origin test, no per-regime breakdown and no comparison against a naive or ARIMA baseline. Action: implement expanding-window walk-forward evaluation across all 35 lanes and report per-lane MAE with a persistence baseline.

#IssueConsequence and required action

14.5 Scope gaps against the problem statement

  • Alternative employment is asserted, not computed. §9.6 recommends evaluating another trade lane during soft windows, but no module actually searches the other 34 lanes for a better netback. This is the most valuable missing capability and a natural next module: for each idle window, maximise rate − voyage_cost across all reachable lanes.
  • No ballast or repositioning model, so idle-management cost is invisible and the recommendation is qualitative.
  • No inventory or lay-time integration — the model assumes cargo is ready when the ship arrives.
  • No multi-leg or transhipment routing. Every lane is direct, so the option of moving via a transhipment hub (which is how several of these cargoes actually move) is absent.
  • No emissions or CII dimension. Fuel is costed but not carbon-costed; increasingly material to charter decisions.
  • No counterparty or credit assessment. Charterer quality is outside the charter/spot framing entirely.
